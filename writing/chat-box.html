The assistant I want isn't a chat box

October 27, 2026 · about 8 minutes

I've had useful conversations with AI about finding my next job, renovating our house, buying a car seat, and planning for retirement. In any one of those conversations it can feel like having someone thoughtful working alongside me. Then I close the window, and I'm still the person holding everything together.

I have to remember which projects need attention, notice when something changes, find the conversation where we decided something, and explain what has happened since. The answers are excellent. Keeping my life moving is still my job.

That gap is what led me to build an assistant of my own: something that keeps the context, advances the work while I'm busy elsewhere, and brings me decisions it has already helped prepare, along with a place where I can see that work, judge its priorities and steer it.

A chat box is a fine way to talk to that assistant. I don't think it should be the place the whole relationship lives.

What I want from an assistant

I use Claude and ChatGPT every day. Chat is an unusually flexible interface: I can arrive with a vague question, work through an idea, push back on an answer, and change direction halfway through. I want to keep all of that. But when I think about an assistant I'd trust with an ongoing role in my life, I want four more things.

  1. Know what's happening. What did we decide about the renovation? Did the recruiter reply? Which deadline is real and which one did I invent for myself? It should keep its information current and keep a record of our decisions that I can read and correct. Picking a project back up shouldn't mean reconstructing the last three weeks.
  2. Understand what matters to me. Knowing I'm looking for a job is a start. Knowing what I want from it, what I'll compromise on, and which attractive roles would take me the wrong direction is what makes the help useful.
  3. Recognize my blind spots. I like forward momentum. I can get very good at answering a question before I've checked whether it was the right question. An assistant that knows me should catch that.
  4. Take initiative and follow through. Keep watching. Do the prep. Bring something back only when there's a decision, a recommendation or a task that needs me, and keep doing it when I'm busy, distracted or tired. Anyone who's married knows the difference between doing what your spouse asks and noticing what needs doing before they have to ask. Just ask my wife which one she'd like more of.

Taken together, that is a much bigger ambition than answering whatever I happen to type. I want an assistant that uses what it knows about me to help me become the person I'm trying to be.

My life happens in parallel. A conversation doesn't.

That ambition changes what I need from the interface.

The job search doesn't pause while I'm dealing with the house. A financial decision can sit unresolved while a dozen newer conversations push it out of view. Family, health, money, work and friends stay important even when I haven't mentioned them lately. A conversation has an order. My responsibilities have relationships, deadlines and competing claims on my attention.

I need to open something and see the situation: what changed, what's progressing, what needs me, what can wait. I want to open a decision and find the recommendation, the evidence and the history together, and once I've answered, I want the assistant to carry on.

That's why I'm building toward a dashboard rather than a thread. The dashboard doesn't create memory or initiative. Those come from connected information, a maintained record, and agents that run between conversations. What the dashboard gives me is a way to see and direct that work. Without it, I could have a capable system behind the scenes and still spend my mornings piecing together its messages.

Chat stays where I go to think out loud, explore something new, or explain a complicated change. It's the terminal now, not the home screen.

My first attempt

The Assistant Dashboard is a rough first attempt at this. It's one page that puts every project in a fixed place and collects the items waiting on me. I can open an item, read what the assistant has prepared, and reply from the page. It's two months old and has been through about a hundred changes, and I don't yet love it.

I'm still learning what deserves space, what deserves urgency, and what should disappear until it matters. Early on, every overdue item turned red, including deadlines I'd casually set for myself, like "pick an estate attorney." Within a week, red meant nothing. Now only a deadline with a real consequence, a deposit due or a cancellation window closing, gets to be red.

A few pieces already show me why this is worth pursuing.

Job Radar: learning what I value. Every morning it reads the product management openings at a few dozen companies I'm interested in and scores each one against a written description of what I want next. The strongest fits reach me with a line on why. I mark each one Interested or Not for me, sometimes with a note.

The most useful part of building it was writing that description. "Find me an interesting product job" left too much unsaid. I had to write down what I was actually optimizing for: room to originate ideas, how close the team is to AI, whether the company has momentum, pay, hours, and the tradeoffs among them. That made my own thinking clearer, and my feedback now gives the system something better than a list of titles. It gives it examples of how I decide.

Project Books: continuity beyond a conversation. Every project, from the renovation to the job search to a dinner planner, has one Notion page with the same four parts: a brief, a decision log, open questions, and a log of what's been done. An agent reads the book before it works and writes a line when it finishes. When I come back to a project, I open a fresh conversation and point it at the book, and the context has a durable home outside the chat.

This isn't perfect memory. A record can be incomplete or wrong. But I can read it, correct it, and see exactly what the assistant is working from, and that matters as much as remembering.

Purchase Advisor: catching my blind spots. This is the example that feels most personal. I enjoy research. Comparing options feels productive, and once I start, the decision quietly becomes which one should I buy. I found myself deep in car research for a car that doesn't need replacing until next summer.

So the advisor has a job before it compares anything: ask whether I need the thing, why now, and what happens if I wait, and ask again if the conversation starts gathering momentum toward a purchase. "Wait" is a useful recommendation. It's also the one I'm worst at reaching when I'm enjoying the research. It's a narrow version of the assistance I want: something that knows a pattern in my behavior and helps me work around it.

Some of the projects are just for fun. The Concert Radar reads the venue newsletters I never open and flags artists I'd want to see while the presale is still ahead, so I pay face value instead of resale. Help keeping up with life should leave room for the good parts of it.

Initiative needs boundaries

Giving an assistant initiative means deciding where that initiative ends.

In an early version, recommendations sometimes showed up in a project's decision log as though I'd made them. Even when the recommendation was reasonable, the record was wrong, and it blurred the line between the assistant's view and my choice. That led to a clearer rule: it can prepare a recommendation, but any change to a decision or a preference waits for my approval.

The same principle runs through the rest of the system. It drafts messages; I review and send them. It researches purchases; I decide whether to spend. It prepares recommendations; I make the calls. These are written instructions, not technical locks, and trust grows as I watch how reliably they're followed. The full set is on AI System.

A personal board of directors

At Kellogg, I joined a program called PBOD: Personal Board of Directors. I was matched with three other students, and we met biweekly for a year to be sounding boards for one another.

We could bring whatever was on our minds. For most of us, that meant careers: which internship to take, which job to pursue, what we wanted to do after business school. As we got closer, the conversations widened. People shared personal struggles, challenging family dynamics, financial concerns. We learned more about the lives those career decisions had to fit into.

It's been about a decade since my MBA, and I still think about that group. There was something valuable about having a regular, safe place to get perspective from people who had come to know me and care about how things turned out.

Over time, you didn't have to explain everything from the beginning. The people around the table knew some of your history. They understood what mattered to you. They could bring a different perspective to a decision while keeping your well-being at the center of it.

That experience has stayed with me as I think about what I want from an AI assistant. The relationships were what made it special, but the structure is the model: a board, not a chat. I've already had one-off conversations with AI about my bloodwork, our spending, retirement savings and the range of financial futures in front of us. I want each of those to become a seat at the table.

  • A health seat that follows my bloodwork and wearable data over time, notices changes, and prepares the questions before a doctor's appointment instead of after.
  • A finance seat that keeps spending and savings connected to our goals, updates the retirement projections as circumstances change, and shows how a single decision shifts the range of outcomes.
  • A relationships seat that helps me follow through with the people I care about when work and family crowd everything else out.

Like a real board, they'd disagree. The finance seat wants me to save more while the relationships seat sees a chance for a trip with close friends. A career move improves our finances and leaves less time with my kids. That's where knowing my values stops being a nice-to-have: I want the board to make those tradeoffs visible, argue each side, and help me decide what fits the life I want. I still get the final vote.

Unlike the Kellogg version, this board never adjourns. It keeps paying attention to the things I've said matter while I'm busy with everything else, and it brings me the decision when there is one.

Something everyone could have

My system is a few early pieces of that picture, and making the whole thing reliable will take real work. But using those pieces has changed what I expect an assistant to be.

Assembling one is also a different thing from using one. Most people shouldn't have to configure scheduled jobs, design a memory system, or build a dashboard to get this kind of help. Those are problems a product could take on.

What I want to open each morning is a clear view of the things I care about, with useful work already underway and a few decisions waiting for me.
