Purchase Advisor

A Claude skill that pressure-tests whether to buy at all, then finds the best real value and recommends. Advisory only: it never buys. I make every purchase myself.

What happened → what I changed

What happened. I asked it to compare rotating car seats and it dutifully compared rotating car seats. Good brief, wrong question: whether we needed one at all before the second baby was the real decision, and nobody had asked it.

What I changed. Anything over $250 now starts with a premise check (needed? why now? what if I wait?) before any research, and the skill is told to raise those questions again whenever the conversation builds momentum toward a specific model. Sometimes the right output is "wait."

What it does

It's a skill rather than a scheduled agent, so it applies anywhere in Claude the moment I'm considering a purchase. For anything over $250 it starts with a premise check: is this needed, why now, and what happens if I wait or do nothing. It revisits those questions when the conversation starts building momentum toward a specific option, and says plainly if the path no longer makes sense. Under $250 it's a one-line sanity check.

If the premise holds, it defines value as lifetime usefulness divided by total cost of ownership (price, consumables, maintenance, time, resale), researches three tiers (a value pick, a sweet spot, and a buy-it-once option) from independent long-term testing and owner reports, checks price history, refurbished and used options, model cycles and warranty, and then recommends. The recommendation leads with the verdict: buy, wait, or don't. Then the top pick and runner-up, a target price, what would change the call, and any rationalizations it caught me making. It gives me links. I click buy, or I don't.

Why I built it

Purchases come up ad hoc, and most of the work of a good one is deciding whether to make it at all. I wanted the research without the momentum: a second opinion that's allowed to say "wait," built into the tool I was already going to ask. And it had to be unable to spend money, because the useful version of an assistant is one I can trust with more each month, and that trust starts with the card staying in my wallet. (Unable by instruction and by design, not by a technical lock: the skill stops at a recommendation and gives links, and no payment details are stored where it could use them.)

How it's built

  • One skill file. The whole thing is a written procedure Claude loads when a purchase comes up: the four steps above, the $250 threshold, and the output format. There is no code, no schedule, and no database. The decision log for it lives in a Notion Project Book like every other project.
  • A written point of view on value. Worth paying more for: durability and repairability, things used daily, kid safety, and anything that saves future decisions. Not worth paying for: brand premium, features I won't use, newest-model status. These are first guesses; the skill is told to tune them from my calls.
  • The format is the product. Verdict first, evidence underneath, which is the same pyramid-principle rule every recommendation in my system follows. A brief I can act on in thirty seconds beats a comparison table I have to read.

Skill files are plain Markdown. If I publish mine, it'll be on GitHub.

What I learned

"Wait" is a legitimate answer, and it's the one I'm worst at reaching alone. The LinkedIn Premium brief didn't pick a plan; it laid out what I'd lose by cancelling and whether that mattered this month. That framing, cost of waiting versus cost of acting now, is the one I skip when I'm the only person in the conversation.

Rules you write for an agent are rules for yourself. Writing down where I'll happily overspend and where I refuse to turned out to be the hard part, and it's still open. The skill can't be more honest about value than I am.
