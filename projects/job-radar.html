Job Radar

LinkedIn has hundreds of PM postings and no idea what I want. Job Radar pulls PM roles straight from the career pages of a few dozen companies I'm interested in, scores each one against a rubric of what I'm seeking next, and sends me only the strongest fits. I spend my time on the five roles worth a look, not the five hundred that aren't.

TL;DR

  • Saves me an hour a week. I'm in no hurry to find the next role, but the rare ideal one can show up any day, and I didn't want to spend an hour a week scanning career pages to catch it.
  • Straight from the source. Every morning it pulls new postings from each company's own career page, so nothing is days old or buried under hundreds of listings that aren't for me.
  • Scored against what I want. A rubric I wrote scores each role 1 to 5. Company momentum and work-life balance are gates: fail either and the role can't score above a 3. Among the rest, the room to originate product bets weighs most, then how central AI is to the team, then level. Only the 4s and 5s reach my dashboard. The rest wait for a Sunday summary.
  • It learns from me. I mark each role Interested or Not for me, sometimes with a note, and those notes get folded back into the rubric. Over time the scoring drifts toward my judgment.

Early iterations

1. Strong on paper, wrong for me

What happened. The first rubric described the jobs I'd like. The first sweep read 75 open roles and flagged 8 as Strong, and when I read those eight, several were wrong for me: big shared surfaces where the PM is a traffic cop, titles that sounded senior but owned no strategy.

What I changed. I rewrote the rubric around what I was actually optimizing for: space to originate product bets, AI centrality, momentum, plus the constraints I'd been avoiding writing down (pay floor, hours, work-life balance). Re-scoring the same roles gave 5 Strong, 14 Maybe and 52 Pass, and the Strong list was one I'd act on.

2. The Dublin problem

What happened. A Staff PM role at Coursera showed up as Strong on my dashboard (the screenshot above). It was based in Dublin, with no mention of remote. The work was a great fit, which is why the rubric liked it, but there's almost no chance of doing that job from Denver.

What I changed. Location is now two rules instead of one. A US-based role is a flag, never a disqualifier, because I'd like to be the candidate a hiring manager rescopes an HQ role to remote for. A role outside the US with no remote option is a pass, no matter how good the work is. It doesn't reach the table, let alone the dashboard.

What I learned

  • Writing the rubric was writing the job search. Deciding whether in-office days in another city are a flag or a filter, or whether I'd rather set my own strategy at a struggling company or execute a good one at a growing company, forced decisions I'd been carrying around as vague preferences. The rubric is now the clearest statement I have of what I want next.
  • The loop matters more than the model. The scoring was mediocre on day one and is good now, and the model didn't change. What changed is that every time I disagreed with a score, I turned the disagreement into a rule.
  • Flags beat filters. My first instinct was to hide roles in other cities. Keeping them visible with a capped score leaves room for the conversation I actually want to have, which is whether the role could be done remotely.

Under the hood

  • Fetch. A private repo with one adapter per job-board platform (Greenhouse, Lever, Ashby, Rippling, Workday, Eightfold) and a scraper for the one company that publishes no feed. A GitHub Action runs the fetch daily and commits new postings as plain JSON, so the history is a git log. Fetching is split from scoring because scheduled Claude runs can't open arbitrary websites without an approval nobody is there to give, and because each half is easier to test on its own.
  • Score. A scheduled Claude task reads the day's file and RUBRIC.md, scores each role 1 to 5, and adds anything 3 or higher to a Notion table with one line on why it would be a good fit for me. The rubric checks two gates first, company trajectory and work-life balance, then ranks what's left on room to originate product bets, closeness to AI, level and problem space. Most of the specific rules came from my notes on real postings: "traffic cop" seats on shared surfaces cap at 3, roles outside the US with no remote option are a pass, and director titles are scored on strategy ownership rather than passed on title.
  • Show. One Notion database: role, company, score, why, status, my notes, location, link. A saved view feeds the tile on my Assistant Dashboard and the Sunday review. Everything reads through saved views because Notion's row-query API has a workspace quota and views don't.
  • Learn. Daily runs read my Interested / Not for me calls and notes, and a Sunday item asks whether any pass was wrong. A full re-score in the first week moved a few roles between Strong and Maybe and found nothing it had wrongly passed on.
